How Bonus Tax Works in Ontario

When your Ontario employer pays you a bonus, they must withhold income tax, CPP contributions, and EI premiums before the money reaches your account. Unlike your regular paycheque — which is taxed based on a flat annual projection — a bonus triggers a different calculation under the CRA T4032 annualization method.

Why the Withholding Looks High

Your employer doesn't apply a flat percentage to your bonus. They combine it with your regular pay period income, project that total over the full year, and determine which marginal tax bracket applies to the extra income. That marginal rate is then applied to the entire bonus in a single pay period. If your salary places you in the 20.5% federal bracket, your bonus is withheld at 20.5% federal plus Ontario provincial tax plus CPP plus EI — all stacked on top of each other. That stacking, not a special bonus rate, is why a $10,000 bonus might deposit only around $6,300.

CRA T4032 Annualization Method

The method works in five steps:

  1. Annualize your regular pay period income (e.g., bi-weekly pay × 26)
  2. Calculate federal + Ontario tax on that annualized income
  3. Add the bonus to the annualized total
  4. Recalculate tax on the combined amount
  5. The difference between steps 4 and 2 is the income tax withheld from your bonus
Tax on bonus = Tax(annualized salary + bonus) − Tax(annualized salary)

Your bonus is withheld at your marginal rate — the rate on your top dollars of income. It's not spread across lower brackets the way your annual income actually is. Only the income above each bracket threshold is taxed at the higher rate when you file. The annualization method concentrates the full marginal effect into one pay period, which makes the withholding appear high.

Withholding Is Not Your Final Tax Bill

What your employer deducts is a prepayment — not the final determination of what you owe. When you file your T1 return, your actual tax is calculated on total annual income after all credits and deductions. RRSP contributions, the Basic Personal Amount ($16,452 federal, $12,989 Ontario for 2026), and other credits all reduce your actual tax — but they may not be reflected in the employer's withholding unless you've filed an updated TD1. If your employer withheld more than your actual liability, the CRA refunds the excess. If too little was withheld, you owe the difference at filing.

CPP and EI on Bonuses

Both are subject to annual maximums that directly affect how much comes off your bonus. CPP1 applies at 5.95% on earnings up to $74,600 (YMPE), max contribution $4,230.45. CPP2 applies at 4% on earnings between $74,600 and $85,000 (YAMPE), max $416.00. EI applies at 1.63% on insurable earnings up to $68,900, max $1,123.07. If your year-to-date salary already exceeds these ceilings, no further CPP or EI is deducted from your bonus. If you're below, they apply only to the portion within remaining room. Ontario surtax may also apply if your basic Ontario tax exceeds $5,818 — the calculator handles this automatically.

What Happens at Tax Time

When you file your T1 return, the CRA calculates your actual tax on your full-year income. The total your employer withheld throughout the year — from both regular pay and bonuses — is credited against that amount. If the total withheld exceeds what you owe, you receive a refund. If it falls short, perhaps because you have other income sources, you pay the difference.
